Reflection project · 7 November 2026 · Research and implementation plan

Aged silvered glass and Victorian mirror ornament

The mirror should read first as an old, polished object: reflective silver beneath thick beveled glass, surrounded by substantial gilt metalwork. Age needs a location, a scale and a material response. The next improvement should come from those relationships, rather than increasing the amount of noise.

Latest implementation: the camera-dominant shared radiance field has been replaced by distinct glass, polished-silver and scattered-silver responses. Each captured frame is decoded once into a linear-light mip pyramid. Intact polish resolves subdued detail; broader returns sample a coarser footprint. Front glass has its own much weaker camera response and preserves glints over missing silver. A second internal return now intersects and samples a different rear position before exiting. The silver's bright reflections retain priority. Separate 768 × 1024 front and rear maps still control normals, thickness, scratches, silver coverage, tarnish and roughness. The approved gold frame, white naturally edged lace and woven pink cloth remain unchanged. The captured Solitude Interior environment supplies the sheen.

Priority correction: the user explicitly prefers chrome shine and aged silver over a clearly visible face. A deliberate 22% chromium finish blend remains for that appearance. The earlier proposal to remove chromium is therefore deferred. The diagnostic table below records the previous baseline; the remaining plan is research and future work, not a claim that every proposed technique has been implemented. Curvature-aware metal wear, detailed sculpted assets, a full microfacet layer model and calibrated full-room reflection remain outstanding. The current roughness convolution is an approximation; it is not a measured BSDF or a chemical corrosion simulation.

Implemented layer separation

PBRT's layered-material treatment distinguishes interface scattering from transport through the material between interfaces. That separation informs this implementation; the realtime shader uses deterministic reflection cones and two explicit internal returns, not PBRT's stochastic solver. The differing camera weights are deliberate appearance choices because the webcam and bundled HDR do not describe one calibrated scene.

1. Photographic references and their specific uses

The design direction remains an ornate late nineteenth-century hand mirror with foliage, open scrollwork and a cherubic figure. The references serve different purposes; their motifs should not all be combined into one frame. Click each photograph to examine its detail.

The V&A's metalworking guide distinguishes casting, repoussé, chasing and engraving. The proposed frame is a cast copper-alloy object with a gilt appearance and chased detail. Relief should look formed into metal; fine veins need not be separate raised wires.

The Walters mirror of about 1900–1905 is a useful style boundary: its catalogue distinguishes Art Nouveau enamel from a Louis XVI revival handle. “Victorian” does not specify one decorative vocabulary. Keep our foliate, figural composition coherent rather than adding every period motif encountered.

3. Build one coherent aged-glass material

Choose the backing before choosing the damage

Use silvered glass as the working material. Williamstown Art Conservation Center documents the nineteenth-century coexistence of silver and tin–mercury mirrors, and distinguishes warm tarnish in silvered mirrors from the blue-toned deterioration of tin–mercury mirrors. Those are different material histories. The photograph alone does not establish a particular object's backing chemistry. This project chooses a warm aged-silver appearance rather than combining both processes. Conservation reference, pages 24–25.

Schematic only; layer widths and ray angles are exaggerated for legibility.

Separate four fields with different optical jobs

  1. Surviving silver: a coverage field sampled at the ray's rear-surface intersection. Complete loss should remove that part of the silver return, reveal the dark backing and retain the front glass reflection. Partial edge coverage should be antialiased, not a semitransparent gray coating across the image.
  2. Tarnish around surviving silver: local absorption/color and roughness variation, related to nearby damage but not identical to it. Avoid bright bronze-colored holes. The Canadian Conservation Institute describes silver tarnish as silver sulfide; do not label it generic iron rust.
  3. Front-surface wear: sparse short scratches and overlapping curved wiping paths sampled at the entry surface. They should perturb normals and spread a reflected light, so visibility depends on lighting. Remove the current unconditional additive scratch brightness.
  4. Large-scale glass variation: a very low-amplitude, smooth height field, with normals derived from its derivatives. Constrain it using a reflected straight-line target. The pane should not read as a convex funhouse mirror or rippling water.

Use organized, unequal wear instead of evenly distributed speckles

Create a deterministic mask generator in physical pane coordinates. Bake the broad fields once, using an actual distance-to-rim map and a few authored damage zones. The current normalized elliptical radius gives different physical band widths on the long and short axes. Evaluate distance to the modeled contour instead.

These are proposed artistic starting ranges, not measurements of the reference objects. Tune the shape and distribution before increasing coverage. Avoid mandatory bottom-heavy damage, because that would import a characteristic associated with amalgam deterioration into the chosen silver model.

Store coverage, tarnish amount and local roughness in a small linear-data texture, initially 1024 × 1024. Keep front scratches in a separate field. Use mipmaps and derivative-aware filtering, a fixed seed and the same masks after graphics-context recovery. Fine detail should settle into a stable surface at normal viewing size, rather than sparkle as the page resizes.

Move the aging into the optical calculation

Refactor the shader into distinct front-glass, surviving-silver and exposed-backing contributions. Apply rear coverage before calculating the returning radiance. Sample rear wear with the local coordinates of backPoint; sampling it with the front position, as now, places the damage at the wrong depth when the view changes.

Retain the tested Fresnel, refraction and absorption calculation. Replace the decorative chromium contribution on the pane with a silver backing response. The Johnson–Christy measurements supply silver optical constants, while PBRT's conductor model and dielectric model provide the interface treatment. Use the metal's index relative to the glass at that interface. Any RGB reduction remains an approximation; it should be documented and checked against the original clean-slab baseline.

Keep intact areas sharply reflective. Sample a wider reflected cone only where the local roughness requires it. Reuse the center sample elsewhere. Leave a large clear region through the center while letting some small isolated defects break the perfect oval of untouched glass.

Make the glass and frame agree about the room

Use one environment orientation, exposure and light arrangement for the glass and frame. Set Three.js's scene environment rotation from the same parameter that drives the glass's environment lookup. Compare a reflected bright window edge and adjacent dark furnishing in both materials. The curved metal compresses that scene into narrow highlights; the flatter pane should show broader, intelligible reflections.

Remove independent decorative light strips once a shared environment achieves the needed contrast. If an additional reflected light is needed, model it as one source seen by both materials. Keep the blush cloth background and avoid synthetic reflection ramps. Do not introduce global bloom, a uniform gray veil, an oil-slick film or strong rainbow bevels to manufacture shine. The rainbow-like marks in one reference photograph are not enough evidence to infer a specific optical mechanism.

The camera remains a single SDR image plane, so it cannot supply the user's complete HDR room or binocular viewpoint. This iteration improves material appearance around that constraint; it does not make face enhancement or head tracking a dependency.

4. Rebuild the frame as a coherent casting

Establish a hierarchy before sculpting details

Keep the oval, a clearly defined bezel, a foliate crest, two principal side sprays and a figure-bearing neck. Replace the evenly spaced leaf-and-scroll ring with a few connected compositions that follow the oval. Let small leaves, beads and rope borders support those larger forms. An open region should have a deliberate outline and believable metal bridges around it.

Use the Vienna and Showtime objects for the overall figural/openwork direction. Use the Cooper Hewitt and Morphets close-ups for relief depth, continuity and tool-mark scale. Retain a warm brass/gilt finish; the silver reference informs shape and workmanship.

Build continuous volumes and genuine negative space

  1. Primary sculpt: create connected subdivision-ready meshes for the crest, side sprays and handle neck. Give leaves finite thickness, rolled lobes and undercuts. Broaden scroll roots where they join the casting; taper them as they curl. Avoid constant circular tubing for every scroll.
  2. Putto: retain one reaching figure integrated into the neck. Block its head, chest, pelvis and bent limbs as one continuous form, with fuller childlike proportions. Check its silhouette in neutral clay at the final display size before adding hair or facial marks. The present slender, segmented figure is a placeholder, not a finished sculpt.
  3. Face and feathers: use shallow eyelids, a continuous brow/nose/cheek transition, grouped hair locks and overlapping rows of feathers. Read the 1887 reverse as a study of low relief, not a template to paste over the glass.
  4. Chased detail: place fine veins, short tool impressions and stippled ground into normal/roughness detail. Keep silhouette-changing features in geometry. A leaf vein should usually be a shallow modeled or baked ridge/groove, not a separate thick wire.
  5. Assembly: show a narrow seating seam between bezel and glass, a believable rim cross-section and local contact shadows. Avoid dark outlines painted around every ornament.

Use an offline sculpt and retopology workflow for the figure and major ornaments, then import a local GLB with Three.js's existing GLTFLoader. This adds no runtime framework. If the local modeling toolchain is unavailable, author connected control meshes directly; do not substitute increasingly elaborate collections of ellipsoids. Keep the procedural code for borders and repeated small moldings where repetition is appropriate.

Place wear where handling and construction make sense

Replace absolute Z-based wear with local cavity, curvature and an authored contact mask. The grip and exposed ridges should carry handling polish and softened detail; sheltered grooves should retain deposits. Distinguish surviving gilt, exposed copper-alloy substrate and dirt. Pure gold itself does not corrode, as the CCI guide explains, so the gold finish should not receive an undifferentiated rust layer.

Use texture channels for cavity and wear, with actual shadowing for larger recesses. Reduce sharpness of genuinely worn geometry at contact points. Do not imitate wear merely by darkening the color while leaving every crest and edge razor-sharp. Start with restrained brown recesses and rubbed warm highlights; avoid generalized green verdigris.

6. Acceptance criteria

  1. Material at normal size: with the camera off, intact silver, tarnished silver, backing holes and front glass are distinguishable. Enlarged views reveal extra detail without making the normal view resemble dirt sprinkled over a photograph.
  2. Stable depth: damage stays attached to the object when the video changes. In a controlled oblique test, rear silver damage and front scratches exhibit the displacement expected from their different depths.
  3. Lighting response: wiping marks become visible near a reflected source and subside elsewhere. No self-luminous scratch lines; no unrelated pixel brightening.
  4. Material dominance: even a black-to-white camera change must preserve the chrome reflection's light/dark structure. The live contribution remains correctly proportioned and visibly integrated with the material; local source changes stay local. Clean optical energy checks remain independent of this art direction. A full-loss fixture is implemented; quantitative angle-dependent wear validation remains future work.
  5. Frame construction: the neck reads as a substantial continuous casting, with a small cherub in shallow relief. Leaves have coherent lobes and roots, scrolls have changing cross-sections, and dark cavities agree with actual geometry. Evaluate against references in clay and metal, not only against the previous screenshot.
  6. Responsive composition: review 1280 × 900, 390 × 844, a narrow 320-pixel viewport and phone landscape. The lace remains a border, the mirror stays the dominant object, and there are no new panels or headings on the mirror page.
  7. Performance and lifecycle: measure representative hardware, targeting a 16.7 ms desktop and 33 ms mobile frame budget as goals rather than current claims. Preserve camera cancellation, Escape shutdown, no microphone, no uploads and context restoration. Check that fine patterns do not shimmer at DPR 1 and 2.

Automated checks establish optical and functional behavior. Perceptual approval comes from the reference comparisons at both full size and close range. A clean clay model, a convincing unaged material and a well-placed aging field are separate review gates.
